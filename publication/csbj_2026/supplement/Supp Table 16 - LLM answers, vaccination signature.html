Executive summary

The strongest biological theme is an innate myeloid program that brings together particle uptake, granule contents, and the machinery that processes engulfed material (HCK, SYK, NCF1, TCIRG1). Alongside it is a program for sensing and responding to inflammatory cues, including cytokine receptors and inflammasome components (CSF3R, IL17RA, NOD2, CASP1). Actin remodeling, membrane traffic, and lipid metabolism help explain why these functions appear together. The surprise is the strength of the neutrophil granule signature alongside features shared with monocytes and macrophages: the list reads more clearly as a map of myeloid functions than as a fingerprint of one cell type.

C1 — Granules and acidic compartments

This community brings together granule-associated proteins and the machinery for acidifying and digesting material in intracellular compartments. Proton-pump subunits help establish the acidic environment in which lysosomal enzymes break down proteins and other cargo (TCIRG1, ATP6V0A1, CTSD, GAA). This is not in the network, but neutrophils can deliver granule contents into phagosomes as well as release them outside the cell. The distinction matters: a strong granule signature could reflect what the cells are equipped to do, rather than show that they released their granules.

C2 — Inflammatory sensing and response

Cytokine receptors point to cells equipped to receive several different signals, from granulocyte growth and survival cues to interferons and IL-17 (CSF3R, IFNGR2, IL17RA). Intracellular sensing and inflammatory processing provide a second thread: NOD2 can signal in response to bacterial cell-wall fragments, while CASP1 and PYCARD point to inflammasome machinery. The programmed cell-death genes add another possible response to stress (APAF1, CASP10), but they do not all belong to the same pathway. What stands out is the breadth of response machinery, not evidence for one particular cytokine or microbial trigger.

C3 — Engulfment and the oxidative burst

Here the biology moves from recognizing a particle to building a compartment around it. Kinases relay signals from uptake receptors, and actin regulators help push the membrane around the cargo (HCK, SYK, VAV1, PAK1). A separate set of proteins helps assemble the NOX2 oxidase that generates superoxide (NCF1, NCF2, NCF4). That makes this community a natural partner to C1: the same engulfed particle can encounter both oxidants and granule-derived enzymes, although having the machinery for both does not establish that either response occurred.

C4 — Membrane supply, glycosylation, and metabolism

This is the most varied community: it combines sugar metabolism, protein glycosylation, Golgi traffic, and lipid metabolism rather than pointing to one metabolic switch. Sugar-handling enzymes and early secretory traffic are represented alongside enzymes that make or turn over sphingolipids (NAGK, NPL, KDELR1, RAB1B, SPTLC2, GBA1). A plausible common thread is the upkeep of membranes and membrane proteins, including the receptors and compartments on which phagocytes rely. The coenzyme A biosynthesis genes (PANK2, PPCDC) broaden the metabolic picture, but do not by themselves make a case for a specific metabolic adaptation.

C5 — Actin remodeling at the cell surface

The clearest signal here is the machinery that builds and shapes branched actin at the cell edge (ACTR3, CYFIP1, CORO1B, CAPZB). A crawling cell and a cell wrapping its membrane around a particle face a similar mechanical problem, so this community fits naturally with C3. The growth-signaling and redox genes are less specific; they could reflect the broader state of the cell rather than a single program that drives movement.

C6 — Small-molecule sulfation

Three closely related sulfotransferases account for much of this signal (SULT1A1, SULT1A3, SULT1A4). There is little here to explain why sulfation would accompany the main phagocyte program, so I would leave it as a separate clue until the sample context is known.

Putting it together

The proton pump subunits and lysosomal membrane proteins add a distinct clue: the list captures machinery for regulating the environment inside degradative compartments as well as the enzymes that work there (TCIRG1, ATP6V0A1, ATP6V0D1, LAMP2).

C2 — Inflammatory sensing and response

These genes describe cells equipped to receive cytokine signals, recognize inflammatory cues, and respond through several routes (CSF3R, CSF1R, IL17RA, NOD2). The inflammasome and apoptosis signals are worth separating: CASP1 and PYCARD point toward inflammatory cytokine processing, whereas APAF1 and DFFA point toward apoptotic execution. The apparent IL-6 signature is less specific than it sounds: the genes driving it include receptors for several cytokines, but not IL6 or STAT3, so it is not a strong clue that IL-6 was the stimulus.

C3 — Engulfment and the oxidative burst

The strongest mechanistic pairing here is receptor-driven engulfment and the capacity to generate oxidants in the resulting compartment. SYK and HCK anchor the signaling side, VAV1 and PAK1 connect that signaling to actin remodeling, and NCF1, NCF2, and NCF4 anchor the NOX2 oxidase machinery. That combination explains why annotations named for particular infections or for microglia appear here: the same machinery is used by many phagocytes, so those labels tell us more about the cellular job than about the organism or tissue involved.

C4 — Membrane lipids, glycosylation, and cargo traffic

This is a mixed section, but several pieces make sense together: cells have to make membrane lipids, process glycoproteins, and route cargo between compartments (SPTLC2, MOGS, KDELR1, KDELR2, RAB1B). The most concrete link back to the lysosomal machinery is that GBA1 breaks down a sphingolipid in lysosomes, while M6PR helps deliver soluble hydrolases to those compartments. The smaller CoA and intermediary-metabolism signals (PANK2, PPCDC, HK1) are less readily folded into a single story; they do not by themselves make a case for metabolic reprogramming.

C5 — Actin remodeling at the cell surface

The clearest feature is machinery for building and shaping actin networks near the cell surface (ACTR3, CYFIP1, CORO1B, CAPG). This belongs alongside C3: a crawling cell extending its leading edge and a phagocyte extending a cup around cargo use some of the same actin machinery. The mTOR-related, redox, and cell-death annotations make this section more heterogeneous; they do not resolve whether the actin genes primarily reflect migration, engulfment, or both.

C6 — A small-molecule sulfation signal

Three closely related sulfotransferases account for most of this signal (SULT1A1, SULT1A3, SULT1A4). That is a more specific reading than broad alcohol breakdown, and the shared glycolytic gene TPI1 does little to explain why the sulfotransferases accompany the myeloid genes. If the list came from RNA sequencing, I would check whether reads reliably distinguish SULT1A3 from SULT1A4 before treating them as two separate observations.

Putting it together

The most useful way to connect these findings is to follow the cargo. CD36 is a particularly informative link because it can recognize altered lipids and help phagocytes take up material such as apoptotic debris. At the cell surface, SYK and PAK1 help translate receptor engagement into changes in cell shape, and ACTR3 and ARPC1B provide a direct link to the branched actin that can build a phagocytic cup. Once cargo is internalized, RAB7A connects uptake to late vesicle maturation, while TCIRG1 and ATP6V0D1 anchor the acidification machinery. One detail is not in the network but matters for interpreting this combination: in neutrophils, NOX2 activity can initially raise phagosomal pH, so the oxidase and proton-pump signals should not be read as evidence of simultaneous acidification.

PYCARD provides a different kind of link: it appears with both the engulfment machinery and the inflammasome machinery. That makes inflammatory sensing during cargo handling an interesting possibility, but it does not mean uptake triggered an inflammasome. MAPK1 recurs across several parts of the picture; it is a useful sign that these responses draw on shared signaling machinery, but a less discriminating clue to the initiating event than the granule proteins or the NOX2 components.

1. Revised summary

The network fits the experiment well: people with higher day-28 hemagglutination-inhibition (HAI) titers tended to have a stronger myeloid expression signature in blood at day 3. The original estimate was right about the sample and the likely contributors—blood, with a substantial neutrophil signal and a possible monocyte contribution—but it missed that the comparison was between eventual antibody responders, not simply between inflammatory conditions. What stands out is how much of the signature concerns granules, engulfment, oxidant production, and vesicle handling (CSF3R, SYK, NCF2, TCIRG1), rather than antibody production itself. That makes it an early correlate of the later response, but it does not tell us whether vaccination increased expression of these genes or whether their expression helped produce the antibody response. The first question I would ask is whether higher responders had more circulating myeloid cells at day 3 or whether those cells were in a different functional state.

2. What fits

What is well established. This is not in the network, but human seasonal influenza vaccination studies have shown that early blood transcriptional patterns can correlate with later antibody responses, and that the circulating plasmablast response is commonly most evident around day 7. So it makes sense for a day-3 correlate to look more like an early innate response than a snapshot of antibody-secreting cells. There is another important distinction: day-28 HAI is an endpoint titer, not necessarily a measure of how much vaccination raised the titer. Unless the original analysis accounted for baseline HAI, the signature could partly reflect prior immunity rather than the response generated by this dose.

What is plausible but less established. The granule and lysosome genes in C1 are a good fit for a neutrophil contribution (GCA, CTSD, LAMP2), reinforced by the granulocyte receptor CSF3R in C2. The cytokine and sensing genes in C2 also leave room for a monocyte contribution (CSF1R, CX3CR1, NOD2). C3 and C5 add a more specific functional possibility: receptor signaling, actin remodeling, and oxidant production could reflect differences in how myeloid cells respond to material they encounter after vaccination (SYK, PAK1, ACTR3, NCF2). That is more interesting than a generic inflammation signature, but circulating phagocytes need not have handled vaccine antigen themselves. They could be reporting an innate response occurring elsewhere, including at the injection site or in draining lymph nodes.

3. What does not fit

The strongest surprise is the prominence of neutrophil granule genes. A large part of that signal could come from differences in cell abundance, redistribution after vaccination, or differences in which cells made it into the assayed blood fraction. Conversely, if the signature persists within neutrophils after accounting for those factors, it becomes much more interesting as a marker of cell state. The granule genes alone cannot distinguish those possibilities, much less establish that degranulation occurred.

C6 is harder to connect to the antibody response. Its signal largely rests on three closely related sulfotransferases (SULT1A1, SULT1A3, SULT1A4); I would check probe specificity or read assignment, depending on the platform, before interpreting it as a coordinated response. I would also set aside the literal readings of the infection- and microglia-labeled pathways: their contributing genes are ordinary phagocyte machinery in this setting. Finally, the apparent IL-6 signal is driven by a mixture of cytokine receptors (CSF3R, IFNAR1, IL17RA), so it gives little reason to single out IL-6 as the mediator.

4. What may be new

Vesicle handling may distinguish antibody responders better than inflammatory signaling alone. The most compelling clue is the connection between C1 and C3: RAB7A links granule-associated and phagocytic compartments, ATP6V0D1 links vesicle pH control to oxidant-related machinery, and NCF2 anchors the oxidase side. C4 extends the picture through GBA1 and M6PR, which connect lysosomal lipid turnover and hydrolase delivery to the broader trafficking program. I am fairly confident that vesicle handling is a coherent part of the signature; I am much less confident that it reflects how vaccine antigen was processed. Its novelty would be showing that a measurable vesicle function predicts antibody outcome within a defined myeloid population, not simply that lysosomal genes recur across annotations.

Inflammatory sensing may be coupled to cargo handling in a subset of responders. PYCARD links the C2 inflammasome machinery to the C3 engulfment signal, while CASP1 anchors the inflammatory side and NOD2 links sensing to the phagocytic compartment. This is a more focused hypothesis than “more inflammation gives a better response”: perhaps an early myeloid state combines uptake with a particular inflammatory output, and that state precedes a larger plasmablast response. Confidence is lower here. Transcript abundance is a poor proxy for inflammasome activity, which depends on protein assembly and cleavage; the expression pattern is a reason to measure that activity, not evidence that it occurred.

5. Priorities

  1. Separate cell abundance from cell state. In a validation cohort, pair baseline and day-3 blood counts and flow cytometry with expression measurements in sorted neutrophils and monocytes. Test whether the signature predicts strain-matched day-28 HAI after accounting for baseline HAI, age, and cell abundance. If the association disappears after accounting for cell counts, the signature may still be useful, but the interpretation shifts from altered function to altered composition.
  2. Test the vesicle-handling hypothesis directly. In fresh day-3 neutrophils and monocytes, use noninfectious fluorescent particles to measure uptake and vesicle pH separately, alongside an oxidant readout. Ask whether any of those functions tracks later HAI within the same cell type, and whether the functional readout adds information beyond cell abundance and the expression signature.
  3. Look for an inflammatory output that precedes the antibody response. Measure caspase-1 activity and mature IL-1β in day-3 myeloid samples, then relate those measurements to day-7 vaccine-specific plasmablasts and baseline-adjusted day-28 HAI. If the transcripts predict HAI but the functional readouts do not, I would keep the signature as a biomarker and drop the inflammasome mechanism as the leading explanation.
