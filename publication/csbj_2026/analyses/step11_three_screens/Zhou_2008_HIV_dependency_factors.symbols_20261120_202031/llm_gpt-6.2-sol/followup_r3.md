## 1. Revised summary

The network is clearly distinguishable from matched random gene lists, and it fits an siRNA screen for host factors required for HIV infection. The earlier estimate of a cell-based genetic screen with an infection-related readout held up; the pairing of CD4 and CXCR4 was the most informative clue. Knowing the experiment makes the host transcription, trafficking, and inflammatory signals easier to interpret, but it does not make every hit an HIV-specific dependency. The striking result is how many subunits of two basic cellular machines—Mediator and mitochondrial complex I—were recovered together. I would now ask which hits reflect something HIV relies on specifically and which reflect changes in cell state or in the assay’s ability to register infection.

## 2. What fits

**Well established.** The receptor signal in C2 is the clearest match. This is not in the network itself, but HIV uses CD4 as a receptor, and CXCR4 serves as a coreceptor for strains that use it. If the screening virus was known to use CXCR4, recovery of both genes is a strong internal check; if not, CXCR4 deserves a different explanation. The other readily recognizable signal is host gene expression: HIV relies on host RNA polymerase II to transcribe its genome. That makes the polymerase-related hits (POLR2H, POLR2K, POLR2L) unsurprising, although those small subunits are shared across nuclear polymerases and do not, by themselves, identify the affected step. The membrane-remodeling signal (VPS4A) also fits established HIV biology, but whether its familiar role in particle release explains the hit depends on what the assay measured and when.

**Plausible, but less specific.** C1 contains a credible connection between inflammatory signaling and viral gene expression (NFKB1, RELA, MAP3K7). NF-κB can promote HIV transcription, but it also regulates broad host responses to stress, so its recovery is more readily explained than the rest of the autophagy signal. C3 likewise fits the experiment at the level of general transcription: recovering a substantial part of Mediator (MED11, MED17, MED26, MED31) points to a shared function rather than an isolated hit. The open question is whether the readout was unusually sensitive to Mediator-dependent transcription or simply sensitive to reduced transcription overall.

## 3. What does not fit

C4 is the biggest surprise. Recovery of several complex I subunits together (NDUFA10, NDUFA6, NDUFS3, NDUFS6, NDUFS7) points to respiratory function more convincingly than any single mitochondrial hit would. What is less clear is why that function mattered in this assay. Cells can remain alive after a respiratory perturbation but grow more slowly, produce less protein, or change how much signal they generate per cell. If the endpoint was a reporter, a reduction in reporter output need not mean a comparable reduction in infection. That makes a general cell-state effect a serious explanation, not a reason to dismiss the result.

C5 is more heterogeneous. The ubiquitin and innate-signaling genes (DTX4, PELI1, UBR5) could reflect a change in the host response, but that is not the same as supplying a function the virus directly needs. The DNA-processing genes (MRE11, POLE) could instead reflect cell-cycle or stress effects, and the nuclear-receptor hits (VDR, RARB) do not yet suggest one clear mechanism. Likewise, the fat-cell differentiation label in C3 mostly reflects the reuse of Mediator subunits; it does not call for an adipocyte explanation. For isolated hits in these groups, the meaning of “validated” matters: replication of an siRNA phenotype is not necessarily confirmation that it was on target.

## 4. What may be new

**A link between energy state and cellular recycling.** The most interesting candidate is the connection between C1 and C4. The bridge genes (PRKAA1, AKT1) link autophagy and growth signaling to metabolic annotations, while the repeated complex I subunits give the respiratory side a clear anchor. One possibility is that respiratory stress shifts how cells balance growth and recycling, producing part of the screen phenotype before overt loss of viability. I have high confidence in the respiratory signal, moderate confidence that it is worth investigating as a cell-state effect, and low confidence that the screen has identified a new HIV-specific mechanism.

**A selective transcriptional effect rather than a general loss of output.** The C3 Mediator hubs make this a stronger candidate than an isolated transcription-factor hit. The bridge through a general initiation factor (GTF2A1) connects C3 to the host transcription machinery in C2, while NFKB1 connects it to inflammatory signaling in C1. The interesting possibility is that some perturbations disproportionately affect stimulus-responsive transcription while leaving basal expression largely intact. The network makes that distinction worth testing, but it does not show which transcriptional outputs were affected.

## 5. Priorities

1. **Separate the complex I signal from general loss of cell function.** In uninfected cells of the same type, use independent reagents against representative complex I hits and measure respiration, live cell number, and a generic reporter per live cell. If reporter output falls along with respiration despite preserved viability, a viability check alone would not have ruled out an indirect effect.
2. **Ask whether Mediator hits preferentially affect inducible transcription.** In uninfected cells, compare a nonviral stimulus-responsive transcription readout with a constitutive readout and a measure of bulk RNA synthesis after perturbing representative Mediator subunits. A broad decrease would favor a general transcription explanation; a selectively blunted response would support the more interesting host-regulation hypothesis.
3. **Test whether respiratory stress and autophagy are functionally connected in these cells.** In an uninfected system, compare a representative complex I perturbation with perturbations of the C1 energy-sensing and autophagy genes (PRKAA1, RB1CC1). Measure autophagic flux—the rate of delivery and breakdown, rather than a static marker—alongside energy state and viability. That would distinguish a shared host response from two processes that merely appeared together in the screen.
