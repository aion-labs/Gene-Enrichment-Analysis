## 1. Revised summary

The network is clearly distinguishable from matched random gene lists, and its emphasis on host transcription, RNA processing, and protein turnover makes sense for an siRNA screen for HIV dependency factors. My earlier estimate was close on the experimental logic—a loss-of-function screen—but missed the defining feature: the endpoint was infection, not simply growth or gene expression. The clearest fit is the machinery that makes and processes RNA (POLR2A, PRPF8, SNRPD3), alongside a distinct nuclear-transport signal (NXF1, NUP214, RANBP2). The proteasome signal is just as coherent at the level of cellular machinery, but harder to attribute to infection rather than cell fitness or the assay readout (PSMA1, PSMC4, PSMD12). The central question now is how much of the list reflects a requirement specific to the infection phenotype, rather than a requirement for the cell to remain healthy and produce a measurable signal.

## 2. What fits

**Well established: HIV depends on host gene-expression machinery.** This is not in the network, but HIV uses host RNA polymerase II to transcribe its integrated genome, and alternative splicing produces different classes of viral RNAs. That makes the pairing of polymerase II and the spliceosome a good fit for the screen (C5 and C1; POLR2A, PRPF8, SF3B1). The important distinction is between a broad process and an individual hit: recovering several spliceosomal subunits makes a shared RNA-processing explanation credible, but it does not tell us whether the phenotype came from altered viral RNA processing or from altered expression of cellular genes.

**Plausible, but less specific: transcriptional control and nuclear traffic.** The Mediator and chromatin regulators in C3 fit an endpoint that depends on transcription, but their presence is not evidence that the screen isolated regulation of viral transcription specifically (MED14, EP300, HDAC1). C4 is similarly coherent: it contains both pore components and transport factors (NUP214, NUP62, RANBP2, NXF1). The distinction that matters here is that different classes of HIV RNA do not all use the same export route. So I would read the NXF1 signal as evidence that host RNA export is represented in the hit list, not as evidence that all viral transcripts use that route or that export was the step affected.

## 3. What does not fit neatly

The surprise is **how much of the proteasome appears**, not that protein turnover appears at all. C2 contains both the core degradation machinery and factors associated with clearing cell-cycle regulators (PSMA1, PSMC4, ANAPC2, UBE2C). That combination makes an indirect explanation especially credible: knockdown could change cell number, cell-cycle state, or the amount of signal produced per cell. Conversely, a phenotype that remained after accounting for those effects would be more interesting than the enrichment alone. If the screen used a reporter, its identity matters here: less reporter signal is not necessarily less infection.

The DNA-repair interpretation also gets narrower on inspection. Much of C5 is the same polymerase II machinery that supports the transcription interpretation, although there is a smaller excision-repair signal (ERCC1, ERCC5, GTF2H2). C6 points to a different kind of DNA processing (MRE11, MUS81); I would not combine the two into a single repair response. Likewise, C7 and C8 are too heterogeneous to support a unified argument for signaling or membrane remodeling (DUSP16, PTPRU, CYBB, OSBPL6). Those genes may be worth revisiting individually if their screen phenotypes were especially reproducible, but the groupings do not explain them.

## 4. What may be new

The first interesting possibility is that **the screen is especially sensitive to the handoff between RNA processing and export**, not just to a general drop in transcription. C1 has a strong spliceosomal core (PRPF8, SNRPD3), while shared genes connect precursor-RNA processing to C4’s export and pore functions (NXF1, NUP98). That makes the handoff a credible feature of the *cellular phenotype*. Confidence that it represents a distinct HIV-specific dependency is low without knowing the hit-level effects and how the endpoint was measured.

A second possibility is that **selective protein turnover and general proteasome capacity contribute differently to the screen phenotype**. C2 contains a recognizable mitotic turnover group (ANAPC2, UBE2C) as well as repeatedly recovered proteasome subunits (PSMA1, PSMA2, PSMA7). The latter also connect to C1 through a MYC-associated gene set, but that overlap could reflect proliferation rather than direct coupling between the spliceosome and proteasome. I’m confident that the two turnover functions are represented; I’m much less confident that they explain the endpoint in the same way. Finding that they have different relationships to cell-cycle state or reporter output would add more than another broad “proteasome dependency” claim.

## 5. Priorities

I’d start with three follow-ups that clarify the original result using the existing screen data and noninfectious controls.

1. **Ask whether the main findings survive reagent-level scrutiny.** Reanalyze the original screen at the individual-siRNA level, if those data are available. Check agreement among independent reagents, shared seed-sequence effects, and whether the spliceosome, pore, and proteasome signals persist after accounting for plate and cell-count effects. This would tell us whether each conclusion rests on several concordant genes and reagents or on a few unusually strong measurements.

2. **Separate RNA handling from reporter behavior.** If the endpoint was reporter-based, compare matched nonviral reporters with and without an intron in uninfected cells, measuring reporter RNA as well as protein output. An intron-dependent difference would point to an RNA-processing sensitivity in the assay; a similar drop in both reporters would favor a broader expression defect. Neither result would establish what happened to viral RNA, but both would sharpen the interpretation of the original endpoint.

3. **Separate proteasome effects from cell fitness and mitotic effects.** In uninfected cells, compare cell number, viability, cell-cycle distribution, and signal per viable cell after perturbing representative core proteasome and mitotic-turnover genes. A common drop that tracks cell fitness would make an indirect explanation more likely; distinct phenotypes would argue against treating C2 as a single effect.

The most useful missing detail is exactly what counted as infection in the original screen, and when it was measured. A reporter-positive fraction and a direct molecular readout can make the same siRNA hit mean quite different things.
