## 1. Revised summary

The network fits an siRNA screen for HIV dependency factors, most clearly in its recovery of nuclear pore proteins and host machinery for transcription and RNA processing. The earlier estimate was partly right: this was a loss-of-function screen in human cells, but the readout was infection, not cellular fitness. That distinction matters because an siRNA can lower an infection signal by disrupting a step in the viral life cycle, by impairing the cell’s ability to support that step, or by lowering the assay signal itself. The nuclear pore group offers the clearest precedent for a direct role in HIV infection, whereas the spliceosome, Mediator, and proteasome groups are more likely to contain a mixture of specific and general dependencies. The surprise is not that these housekeeping systems show up, but that so many components of each show up together.

## 2. What fits

**Well-established biology.** The strongest match is the nuclear pore group (C4). This is not in the network, but HIV-1 capsid engages nuclear pore proteins during nuclear entry, with well-established roles for NUP153 and RANBP2 that can vary with cellular context. That makes this group a more specific fit than the broad transport label would suggest. It does not make every pore hit an entry factor: NXF1 points to cellular messenger RNA export, and depletion of a pore component can affect several kinds of traffic at once.

The transcription and splicing groups (C1, C3, C5) also fit, especially if the endpoint required expression from the viral genome. HIV uses host RNA polymerase II to transcribe its genome and host splicing machinery to produce different classes of viral RNA. The polymerase subunits (POLR2A, POLR2I) and spliceosome components (SNRPA, SF3B1, PRPF8) therefore represent established requirements **at the level of the process**. What is not established by their presence is that HIV has an unusual dependence on each subunit, rather than sharing the cell’s dependence on it.

**Plausible but less specific.** The pore and splicing groups meet at RNA handling (NXF1, NUP214, RANBP2). That is biologically interesting, but it would be a mistake to interpret NXF1 as evidence for the canonical export route used by HIV’s incompletely spliced RNAs: that route uses Rev and CRM1. Similarly, the transcriptional regulators (EP300, HDAC1, MED14) make sense in a screen that depends on viral gene expression, but the list alone cannot tell whether their main effect was on the provirus, on host transcription, or on expression of the assay reporter.

## 3. What does not fit

The **breadth of the proteasome signal** (C2) is the biggest surprise. Recovery of both the core and regulatory cap (PSMA3, PSMB6, PSMC5, PSMD12) looks more like sensitivity to loss of proteasome function than evidence that many separate subunits each have an HIV-specific role. Impaired proteostasis can change cell number, cell-cycle state, or protein output, any of which could depress an infection endpoint. The cell-cycle genes (ANAPC2, UBE2C, PLK1) sharpen that alternative explanation; they do not establish that HIV requires dividing cells.

The **repair signal** is also easy to overread. Much of C5 is carried by polymerase subunits, with TFIIH providing a real connection between transcription and repair (GTF2H2). C6 is a separate, smaller signal involving DNA-break and branched-DNA processing (MRE11, MUS81); it is not independent confirmation that the hits reflect a single repair response. The mitochondrial, phosphatase, and perinuclear membrane groups (C3, C7, C8) could reflect altered cell physiology or less obvious host pathways. Without a coherent effect across independent siRNAs, those smaller groups are hard to distinguish from off-target effects or assay variability.

## 4. What may be new

The most interesting possibility is a **host RNA-processing phenotype shared by some pore and spliceosome hits**. NXF1 connects the splicing and RNA-processing group to the export group, while NUP98 links splicing, nuclear pore, and transcriptional regulation annotations (C1, C3, C4). I have moderate confidence that these overlaps identify a coherent host process worth separating from nuclear entry, but low confidence that they represent a distinct HIV dependency. The useful question is whether the same hits also leave a recognizable signature in host RNA processing in the absence of infection.

A second possibility is that the **proteasome and cell-cycle hits mark a common cell state that changes how the endpoint behaves**. The proteasome hubs establish that this is a complex-wide signal, while ANAPC2 and UBE2C provide a more specific link to timed protein turnover within C2. Several proteasome subunits also connect C2 to the MYC-related annotation in C1, but that overlap does not establish MYC activation. I have higher confidence in a shared turnover phenotype than in a direct viral mechanism. If this group behaves differently from equally growth-impairing knockdowns, that would be the genuinely interesting result.

## 5. Priorities

1. **Separate the pore signal from general transport defects.** Start with the existing data: ask whether the pore hits reproduce across independent siRNAs and whether their effects persist after accounting for cell count and any available reporter controls. In uninfected cells, compare host messenger RNA localization with a general nuclear transport readout. That would help distinguish broadly disruptive pore knockdowns from ones with a narrower host phenotype.
2. **Find out whether the proteasome signal is mostly a fitness or reporter effect.** In matched uninfected cells, measure proteasome function, viable cell number, and baseline reporter output after representative knockdowns. Compare those results with the original screen scores. If the infection scores track reporter loss or declining cell fitness, that parsimoniously explains why so much of the complex appeared.
3. **Distinguish RNA processing from a general loss of transcription.** In uninfected cells, compare host splice-junction usage and nuclear RNA retention with a measure of overall transcription after representative spliceosome, export, and polymerase knockdowns. A processing defect without a comparable drop in total transcription would support a narrower host phenotype; a broad collapse would favor general cellular dependence.

The detail that would most change this interpretation is the original endpoint: a late reporter integrates very different steps from a direct measurement of an early infection event. Knowing what the screen measured, and what it measured in parallel for viability and reporter expression, would tell us which apparent dependencies the original experiment could actually resolve.
