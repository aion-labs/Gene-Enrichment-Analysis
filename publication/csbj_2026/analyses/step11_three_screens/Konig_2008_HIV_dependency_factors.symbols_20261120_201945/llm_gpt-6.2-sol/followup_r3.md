## 1. Revised summary

The network fits an siRNA screen for HIV dependency factors, especially in its emphasis on nuclear transport, transcription, and RNA processing. My original estimate got the loss-of-function format broadly right but missed the endpoint: this was an infection screen, not a screen for cell growth or survival. That distinction matters because silencing a gene can lower the infection readout either by interrupting a step HIV depends on or by making the cell less able to support gene expression or produce the assay signal. The nuclear pore and RNA-processing findings are particularly informative because HIV encounters those systems at several distinct points in its life cycle. The dense proteasome and polymerase findings are equally worth understanding, but broad effects on cell function are a stronger competing explanation there.

## 2. What fits

**Well established.** This is not in the network, but HIV relies on host machinery to bring its replication complex into the nucleus, transcribe the integrated provirus, and process viral RNAs. That makes the nuclear pore group (C4), the transcription groups (C3 and C5), and the spliceosome group (C1) a good biological fit. RNA polymerase II makes viral transcripts from the provirus, and alternative splicing allows HIV to produce different messages from the same primary transcript. The collection of spliceosome components (SNRPA, SNRPD3, PRPF8, SF3B1) therefore makes more sense in this experiment than it did under the original growth-screen hypothesis. What is established is the dependence on the host machinery as a whole, not that each of these factors has an HIV-specific role.

**Plausible, but less well resolved.** The pore hits (NUP153, NUP214, NUP62, RANBP2) are compelling at the level of the cellular structure, but a pore-associated phenotype does not tell us whether the affected step is incoming nuclear traffic, outgoing RNA traffic, or ordinary host transport. NXF1 is an especially useful reminder of this distinction. It is a major host mRNA export receptor, whereas HIV uses Rev and XPO1 to export its unspliced and incompletely spliced RNAs. Its presence alongside spliceosome and pore proteins is therefore evidence that host RNA maturation and export are represented, not evidence that NXF1 carries those Rev-dependent viral transcripts.

The proteasome group (C2) is also plausible, but its interpretation is less specific. Ubiquitin-dependent turnover can change the abundance of proteins that help or hinder infection. Yet the repeated appearance of core proteasome subunits (PSMA1, PSMA3, PSMC5, PSMD12) could just as readily reflect the cost of compromising a machine that the whole cell depends on. The cell-cycle turnover factors (ANAPC2, UBE2C) sharpen that alternative: a change in cell state could change the screen endpoint without identifying a direct viral requirement.

## 3. What does not fit

The biggest trap is to read C5 as evidence that the screen recovered a dedicated DNA repair response to HIV. Much of that group is accounted for by the same polymerase subunits that support transcription (POLR2A, POLR2C, POLR2I, POLR2J). The excision repair factors (ERCC1, ERCC5) are genuine repair clues, but they do not establish a role in integration. The smaller repair group (C6) is distinct: MRE11 and MUS81 suggest processing of DNA breaks or branched intermediates, but the rest of the group does not reinforce a single repair mechanism.

The differentiation labels in C3, the metabolic labels in C2, and the small phosphatase and perinuclear membrane groups (C7 and C8) are weaker clues to HIV biology. They may reflect the state of the particular cells used, or they may be real effects that act indirectly. For an siRNA screen, the key technical distinction is between **on-target depletion that broadly changes cell function** and **an off-target effect of the siRNA itself**. A viability measurement alone would not settle that distinction: cells can remain alive while producing less RNA, translating less protein, or giving a weaker reporter signal.

## 4. What may be new

The strongest lead is the overlap between **RNA processing and export** in C1 and C4. The same export receptor and pore proteins recur on both sides (NXF1, NUP214, NUP62), while the splicing group contains a broad set of core factors. That makes it worth asking whether some hits reflect a change in *which RNAs mature and reach the cytoplasm*, rather than simply a drop in total gene expression. I’m confident that the list captures both host processes; I’m much less confident that the overlap identifies a previously unknown HIV-specific mechanism.

A second lead is the overlap between **transcriptional regulation and splicing**. SNW1 bridges the spliceosome-rich C1 and the transcriptional-regulator group C3, while polymerase subunits connect C1 to C5. These recurring genes make a coupled RNA-production phenotype more plausible than two wholly unrelated effects. By comparison, a distinct role for repair is a lower-confidence lead: the repair enzymes are worth keeping separate from the polymerase-driven signal, but the network cannot tell whether they account for the original phenotype.

## 5. Priorities

I’d start with three controls and host-cell experiments that would make the original infection result easier to interpret, without requiring a new infection assay.

1. **Determine how much of the proteasome signal tracks general cell impairment.** Retest representative C2 hits with independent siRNAs in uninfected cells. At the original assay time point, measure viable cell number, cell-cycle distribution, and output from an unrelated constitutive reporter. A reporter drop in otherwise viable cells would show why normal viability is not enough to infer infection specificity.
2. **Distinguish an RNA-processing defect from a general fall in transcription.** For representative C1 and C4 hits, compare endogenous intron retention and nuclear versus cytoplasmic host RNA in uninfected cells, alongside a measure of total RNA output. Selective changes in processing or localization would support a more specific host-cell phenotype than a uniform reduction in RNA.
3. **Separate the repair signal from the polymerase signal.** Compare depletion of representative repair factors with depletion of a polymerase subunit in uninfected cells, measuring basal transcription and a standard host DNA repair readout separately. That would show whether the two classes produce distinguishable cellular phenotypes before treating the repair annotation as evidence for a separate requirement in the original screen.
