## 1. Revised summary

The network fits the experiment well: people with higher day-28 hemagglutination-inhibition (HAI) titers tended to have a stronger myeloid expression signature in blood at day 3. The original estimate was right about the sample and the likely contributors—blood, with a substantial neutrophil signal and a possible monocyte contribution—but it missed that the comparison was between eventual antibody responders, not simply between inflammatory conditions. What stands out is how much of the signature concerns granules, engulfment, oxidant production, and vesicle handling (CSF3R, SYK, NCF2, TCIRG1), rather than antibody production itself. That makes it an early correlate of the later response, but it does not tell us whether vaccination increased expression of these genes or whether their expression helped produce the antibody response. The first question I would ask is whether higher responders had more circulating myeloid cells at day 3 or whether those cells were in a different functional state.

## 2. What fits

**What is well established.** This is not in the network, but human seasonal influenza vaccination studies have shown that early blood transcriptional patterns can correlate with later antibody responses, and that the circulating plasmablast response is commonly most evident around day 7. So it makes sense for a day-3 correlate to look more like an early innate response than a snapshot of antibody-secreting cells. There is another important distinction: day-28 HAI is an endpoint titer, not necessarily a measure of how much vaccination raised the titer. Unless the original analysis accounted for baseline HAI, the signature could partly reflect prior immunity rather than the response generated by this dose.

**What is plausible but less established.** The granule and lysosome genes in C1 are a good fit for a neutrophil contribution (GCA, CTSD, LAMP2), reinforced by the granulocyte receptor CSF3R in C2. The cytokine and sensing genes in C2 also leave room for a monocyte contribution (CSF1R, CX3CR1, NOD2). C3 and C5 add a more specific functional possibility: receptor signaling, actin remodeling, and oxidant production could reflect differences in how myeloid cells respond to material they encounter after vaccination (SYK, PAK1, ACTR3, NCF2). That is more interesting than a generic inflammation signature, but circulating phagocytes need not have handled vaccine antigen themselves. They could be reporting an innate response occurring elsewhere, including at the injection site or in draining lymph nodes.

## 3. What does not fit

The strongest surprise is the prominence of neutrophil granule genes. A large part of that signal could come from differences in cell abundance, redistribution after vaccination, or differences in which cells made it into the assayed blood fraction. Conversely, if the signature persists within neutrophils after accounting for those factors, it becomes much more interesting as a marker of cell state. The granule genes alone cannot distinguish those possibilities, much less establish that degranulation occurred.

C6 is harder to connect to the antibody response. Its signal largely rests on three closely related sulfotransferases (SULT1A1, SULT1A3, SULT1A4); I would check probe specificity or read assignment, depending on the platform, before interpreting it as a coordinated response. I would also set aside the literal readings of the infection- and microglia-labeled pathways: their contributing genes are ordinary phagocyte machinery in this setting. Finally, the apparent IL-6 signal is driven by a mixture of cytokine receptors (CSF3R, IFNAR1, IL17RA), so it gives little reason to single out IL-6 as the mediator.

## 4. What may be new

**Vesicle handling may distinguish antibody responders better than inflammatory signaling alone.** The most compelling clue is the connection between C1 and C3: RAB7A links granule-associated and phagocytic compartments, ATP6V0D1 links vesicle pH control to oxidant-related machinery, and NCF2 anchors the oxidase side. C4 extends the picture through GBA1 and M6PR, which connect lysosomal lipid turnover and hydrolase delivery to the broader trafficking program. I am fairly confident that vesicle handling is a coherent part of the signature; I am much less confident that it reflects how vaccine antigen was processed. Its novelty would be showing that a measurable vesicle function predicts antibody outcome *within* a defined myeloid population, not simply that lysosomal genes recur across annotations.

**Inflammatory sensing may be coupled to cargo handling in a subset of responders.** PYCARD links the C2 inflammasome machinery to the C3 engulfment signal, while CASP1 anchors the inflammatory side and NOD2 links sensing to the phagocytic compartment. This is a more focused hypothesis than “more inflammation gives a better response”: perhaps an early myeloid state combines uptake with a particular inflammatory output, and that state precedes a larger plasmablast response. Confidence is lower here. Transcript abundance is a poor proxy for inflammasome activity, which depends on protein assembly and cleavage; the expression pattern is a reason to measure that activity, not evidence that it occurred.

## 5. Priorities

1. **Separate cell abundance from cell state.** In a validation cohort, pair baseline and day-3 blood counts and flow cytometry with expression measurements in sorted neutrophils and monocytes. Test whether the signature predicts strain-matched day-28 HAI after accounting for baseline HAI, age, and cell abundance. If the association disappears after accounting for cell counts, the signature may still be useful, but the interpretation shifts from altered function to altered composition.
2. **Test the vesicle-handling hypothesis directly.** In fresh day-3 neutrophils and monocytes, use noninfectious fluorescent particles to measure uptake and vesicle pH separately, alongside an oxidant readout. Ask whether any of those functions tracks later HAI within the same cell type, and whether the functional readout adds information beyond cell abundance and the expression signature.
3. **Look for an inflammatory output that precedes the antibody response.** Measure caspase-1 activity and mature IL-1β in day-3 myeloid samples, then relate those measurements to day-7 vaccine-specific plasmablasts and baseline-adjusted day-28 HAI. If the transcripts predict HAI but the functional readouts do not, I would keep the signature as a biomarker and drop the inflammasome mechanism as the leading explanation.
