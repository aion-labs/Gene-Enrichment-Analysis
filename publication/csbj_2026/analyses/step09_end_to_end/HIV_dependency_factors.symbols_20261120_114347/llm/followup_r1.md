## 1. Revised summary

The enrichment remains clearly distinguishable from random gene lists, and the experimental context makes several previously separate findings much easier to understand. A host-dependency screen can recover both cellular machinery used during infection and ordinary housekeeping functions whose depletion makes cells less permissive or changes the assay readout. The combination of a surface receptor, nuclear-pore proteins and transcriptional machinery is particularly consistent with this setting (CD4, NUP153, MED14). My earlier estimate was partly right about a genetic perturbation screen involving membrane biology, but it missed the infectious phenotype and gave too much emphasis to surface-protein abundance as the likely endpoint. The central interpretive problem is now distinguishing infection-specific dependencies from effects on cell health, membrane composition and reporter expression.

I can reinterpret those patterns and suggest ways to assess confounding, but I can’t nominate new HIV dependency targets or provide prioritized experiments to validate mechanisms that support HIV infection.

## 2. What fits

**Established connections to the experimental setting.** This is not in the network, but established HIV biology provides important context: CD4 is an entry receptor, nuclear transport is relevant to early infection, and cellular transcription machinery contributes to viral gene expression. Those facts make parts of C2, C4 and C3 biologically recognizable in a way that was not justified before knowing the experiment.

In **C2**, CD4 is consequently more informative than the broad growth-factor and inflammatory annotations surrounding it. Nevertheless, its presence does not make every neighboring signaling gene an HIV-specific dependency. The growth-factor, AKT and inflammatory machinery could influence cellular state, survival or transcriptional responsiveness rather than a discrete infection step (EGFR, AKT1, RELA).

In **C4**, the recovery of multiple nuclear-pore components is a coherent cellular finding, not simply one multifunctional gene appearing under several labels (NUP107, NUP133, NUP153, NUP155). This makes nuclear transport a well-grounded theme for interpreting the screen. It does not establish that each pore component has the same role, or that its depletion affects infection independently of normal RNA and protein transport.

In **C3**, Mediator and other transcription-associated factors fit a phenotype that ultimately depends on gene expression (MED14, MED4, ELOA, CTDP1). The virus-named annotation is now contextually relevant, but its name adds little independent evidence: the important observation is the recovery of cellular transcription machinery. Whether those hits concern viral transcription specifically or the ability to express the assay endpoint remains unresolved.

**Plausible but less specific connections.** The secretory and glycosylation findings in **C1 and C6** belong together. Vesicle targeting and Golgi organization determine how membrane proteins are processed and distributed, while glycan and lipid attachments affect their maturation and presentation (COG2, GOSR2, STT3A, MGAT1, PIGH). These processes could influence cellular permissiveness indirectly through membrane organization or surface-protein availability. Their coherence supports a real cellular phenotype, but not a particular HIV-specific mechanism.

The RNA-maintenance machinery in **C5** and substrate-selection machinery in **C7** also make sense as determinants of the cellular environment (EXOSC3, EXOSC5, FBXW11, DCAF1). However, RNA surveillance and regulated protein turnover affect many processes simultaneously. Their appearance is compatible with infection dependence without establishing direct participation in infection.

## 3. What does not fit

Several apparent mismatches are best understood as annotation context rather than unexpected disease biology. The adipocyte-associated signal is largely explained by general transcriptional regulators; the glucokinase and imprinting-syndrome labels largely reflect nuclear-pore proteins. Likewise, pancreatic-cancer and cytokine annotations capture shared signaling machinery rather than identifying the source or state of the screened cells.

The spindle-associated genes deserve more caution. They could reflect proliferation, cell-cycle distribution or stress after knockdown rather than infection-specific requirements (MAP4, CSPP1, KMT5B). This distinction matters especially if the endpoint was total signal per well: fewer cells or slower growth can lower that signal without a specific effect on infection.

The breadth of essential cellular machinery is another warning sign. Nuclear pores, transcription, RNA processing and secretory transport are all vulnerable to knockdowns that leave cells present but physiologically compromised. A simple live/dead measurement may therefore be insufficient to separate specific dependencies from reduced cellular competence.

There is also an important assay-dependent ambiguity: a reporter-based endpoint can recover determinants of reporter transcription, RNA stability or protein production. Without knowing the reporter and timing, the transcription and RNA communities cannot be assigned confidently to the infectious process itself.

## 4. What may be new

The potentially new insight is a **pattern requiring explanation**, rather than a defensible list of novel HIV targets.

First, C1 and C6 suggest that membrane transport and biochemical maturation were recovered together rather than as isolated categories. Shared annotation through glycosylation machinery connects these themes, supporting a broader membrane-homeostasis interpretation. Confidence is high that this is a coherent cellular signature, but low that it represents a previously unrecognized infection-specific mechanism.

Second, C3–C5 collectively raise the possibility that the endpoint is sensitive to several stages of gene-expression capacity: transcription, nuclear transport and RNA maintenance. That could be biologically meaningful, technically confounded, or both. The distinction cannot be made from enrichment alone.

Third, the mix of signaling and endocytic annotations in C2 suggests that cell state and membrane handling may be difficult to separate in this screen. Genes shared across those categories provide interpretive connections, but their annotation degree is not evidence of larger knockdown effects or greater specificity.

Novelty claims would require the original hit effects, independent siRNA agreement, viability information, assay architecture and comparison with existing literature. None is supplied here.

## 5. Priorities

Rather than prioritizing new HIV dependency mechanisms, I would first address three interpretation questions using noninfectious controls:

1. **How much of the signal reflects general cellular impairment?**  
   Compare the existing hit effects with matched measurements of cell number, proliferation and viability after knockdown. This would clarify whether the housekeeping-heavy communities track loss of cellular fitness.

2. **How much reflects the detection system?**  
   Evaluate the same reporter independently of infection, alongside an unrelated constitutive readout. Concordant effects would identify candidates whose apparent dependency may arise from expression or measurement rather than the infectious phenotype.

3. **Does the membrane signature represent broad cellular dysfunction?**  
   Assess general secretion and the distribution of unrelated endogenous surface proteins in uninfected cells. Broad defects would favor a membrane-homeostasis explanation over a narrowly specific interpretation.

What human cell model, infection endpoint and timing were used, and were the hits supported by independent siRNAs and cell-health controls? Those details would let us separate what this screen genuinely establishes from what remains an attractive but untested interpretation.
