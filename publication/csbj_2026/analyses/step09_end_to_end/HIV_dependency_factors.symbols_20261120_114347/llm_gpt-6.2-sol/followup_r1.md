## 1. Revised summary

The network makes good sense for an siRNA screen for human genes required for HIV infection: the host machinery for receptor availability, membrane traffic, and transcription is well represented, and the network is clearly distinguishable from those produced by random gene lists. The strongest match is CD4, while the transcription group recovers host factors that are already annotated for HIV transcription. My earlier guess was right about the perturbation-screen format and the importance of cell-surface biology, but wrong about the endpoint: EGFR is a clue to shared trafficking and signaling machinery, not the receptor that explains the screen. The nuclear pore, RNA decay, and protein turnover groups are harder to assign because knocking down those systems can change the assay endpoint without selectively affecting infection. The key distinction is between an **infection-screen hit** and a **specific host dependency**.

## 2. What fits

**The established biology.** Outside the network, CD4’s role as an HIV entry receptor is established. That makes it a much more informative anchor for C2 than EGFR, even though EGFR appears in more annotations. CD4’s appearance alongside cargo uptake and sorting proteins (AP2M1, HIP1R) also raises a straightforward interpretive question: did some knockdowns change the amount of receptor at the surface rather than the cell’s ability to support a later stage? The screen endpoint alone cannot distinguish the two.

C3 fits for a different reason. HIV depends on host RNA polymerase II for transcription, and the list recovers both general transcriptional machinery (MED14, MED4, MED6) and a smaller group already annotated for HIV transcription (CTDP1, ELOA, ERCC3). That annotation is no longer an incidental label, as it seemed before the assay was known. It is nonetheless evidence of a match at the process level, not proof that every recovered factor acts selectively on viral transcription rather than broadly on transcription in the cell.

**Plausible, but less specific.** C1 and C6 make sense together because protein maturation and delivery determine what is available at the cell surface. The glycosylation factors (DDOST, STT3A, MGAT1) and Golgi machinery (GOSR2, RAB2A, COG2) could therefore influence the screen indirectly through changes in surface composition. The interpretation depends on which cells received the siRNA. If only target cells were depleted, for example, the glycosylation hits would not by themselves show that the incoming virus was made differently. C4 is likewise a plausible fit at the level of nuclear transport, but the recurrence of nucleoporins (NUP107, NUP133, NUP160) does not identify which cargo or transport step accounts for the phenotype.

## 3. What does not fit cleanly

C5 is the clearest challenge to a virus-specific reading. The RNA exosome factors (EXOSC3, EXOSC5, MPHOSPH6) form a coherent group, but the accompanying polymerase I and III subunits point to broader RNA production, not the polymerase II machinery discussed above. If the endpoint was a reporter, a knockdown that changed RNA turnover, protein production, or cell number could change the signal without changing the number of infected cells. The spindle genes in C2 make cell number and cell-cycle state particularly worth checking: a well can contain fewer cells at the endpoint even if the remaining cells show little evidence of acute toxicity.

C7 is harder to read as a unit. Substrate adaptors (FBXW11, DCAF1, KLHDC2) share a role in protein turnover, but that does not imply a common substrate or explain why their depletion scored. And although the GPI-anchor machinery in C6 is unusually coherent (PIGH, PIGY, PIGK), that signal cannot be explained by CD4 itself being GPI-anchored: CD4 is a transmembrane protein. For these groups, reproducibility across independent siRNAs would argue against a sequence-specific off-target effect, but would not distinguish a selective phenotype from a broad cellular one.

## 4. What may be new

The most interesting **cell-biological** question is whether the glycan-processing and Golgi hits converge on a selective change in surface composition or simply impair delivery across many cargos. The evidence for a connection is strong: DDOST, STT3A, and MGAT1 bridge C6’s processing machinery to C1’s secretory biology, and MGAT1 also appears with early secretory components (GOSR2, RAB2A, TMED2). I’m confident that the two groups capture complementary parts of the secretory pathway; I’m much less confident that they point to any one affected surface protein.

A second question is whether the GPI-anchor group represents a phenotype distinct from general glycosylation or secretion defects. The same core factors recur across several annotations (PIGH, PIGY, PIGK). That repetition is not independent evidence, but it does make the cellular process unusually clear. Finally, the exosome group raises a different possibility: the screen may have picked up a specific sensitivity of the assay readout to RNA handling. That would be a useful finding in its own right, even if it narrowed rather than expanded the set of credible dependencies.

## 5. Priorities

I’d follow up the findings that best separate selective cellular effects from general assay effects. These can be tested without virus or viral material; they would clarify the screen without assigning or optimizing new HIV dependencies.

1. **Surface abundance versus total protein.** In uninfected cells, use independent siRNAs to revisit representative glycosylation and trafficking hits. Measure surface and total CD4 alongside unrelated surface proteins, with live-cell counts. A selective surface change, a general loss of surface cargo, and a fall in total protein would support different explanations.
2. **GPI anchoring versus general secretion.** Compare an endogenous GPI-anchored marker with a transmembrane marker and a soluble secreted protein after depletion of GPI-pathway factors. Selective loss of the anchored marker would distinguish an anchoring defect from broader secretory impairment.
3. **RNA handling versus assay output.** In uninfected cells, measure the RNA and protein outputs of a matched nonviral reporter after representative RNA exosome knockdowns, alongside cell counts and a measure of general protein production. If the reporter shifts on its own, that gives a concrete reason to reinterpret the original endpoint before attributing the hit to infection.
