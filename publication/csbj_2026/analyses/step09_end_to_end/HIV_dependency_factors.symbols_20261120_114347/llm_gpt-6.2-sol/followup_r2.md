## 1. Revised summary

The network makes good sense for an HIV host-dependency screen, but it looks like a mixture of HIV-relevant host biology and the normal cellular functions needed to register an infection signal. My earlier estimate was right about a loss-of-function screen in human cells, but wrong to make a surface-protein or growth-factor response the central phenotype. With HIV in view, transcriptional elongation and nuclear transport become more informative than the EGFR label, while the ER–Golgi machinery could matter for several different reasons depending on which cells were depleted and when the endpoint was measured. The most intriguing result is the distinct GPI-anchor signal: it is more specific than a generic secretory-pathway phenotype, yet it is not an obvious explanation for HIV entry. I would treat the list as a map of possible reasons for a lower infection readout, not as evidence that every hit acts directly on the virus.

## 2. What fits

**Well established: HIV depends on host transcription and nuclear transport.** This is not in the network itself, but HIV Tat recruits the host elongation factor P-TEFb to help RNA polymerase II make full-length viral transcripts. That makes the C3 elongation genes (CTDP1, ELOA, ERCC3) biologically relevant, though their presence does not make each one a Tat cofactor. The repeated Mediator subunits (MED14, MED4, MED6, MED7) make a second point: the screen recovered a substantial piece of the host transcription apparatus, not just a few genes from a named HIV pathway. If the endpoint was expression-dependent, some of those hits could reduce the final signal even when earlier steps were intact.

C4 also fits known HIV biology. HIV uses nuclear pore machinery during early infection and a distinct export pathway later to move incompletely spliced viral RNAs into the cytoplasm. Those are different events, and the pore genes here (NUP107, NUP153, NUP155, RANBP2) should not all be assigned to either one. NUP153 has a known connection to HIV nuclear entry; the recurrence of several other pore subunits could reflect the importance of an intact pore rather than a direct role for each subunit. In particular, the cellular mRNA-export annotations should not be read as evidence that this group specifically reports the viral Rev export pathway.

**Plausible, but less directly supported: membrane traffic changes the state of the target cell.** C1 and C2 offer a reasonable route from Golgi traffic to the abundance or distribution of surface proteins (GOSR2, RAB2A, AP2M1). The presence of CD4 makes that interpretation more compelling for an HIV screen than the prominence of EGFR does. But the uptake machinery does not tell us that HIV entered through a clathrin-dependent route; it could instead change how much CD4 is available at the surface. C6 adds another distinction: if only target cells received siRNA and the endpoint captured a single round, altered glycosylation of the *incoming* viral envelope would not explain those hits. Effects on host surface proteins would still be plausible.

## 3. What does not fit

The coherent GPI-anchor signal (PIGH, PIGY, PIGK) is harder to explain than the N-glycosylation signal. CD4 and the canonical HIV coreceptors are not GPI-anchored. If the GPI hits are on-target, they could point to an accessory surface protein or a broader change in membrane organization, but the screen does not identify which. Conversely, the repeated recovery of genes at distinct steps of the same biosynthetic pathway makes this harder to dismiss than a lone siRNA hit.

The EGFR growth-signaling axis (EGF, EGFR, AKT1) and C5 RNA-quality-control machinery (EXOSC3, EXOSC5, MPHOSPH6) are also not obvious HIV-specific dependencies. EGFR-axis depletion could alter proliferation, survival, or transcriptional responsiveness; exosome depletion could change RNA abundance or how much reporter accumulates. C7 is more heterogeneous still: the presence of several ubiquitin-ligase adaptors does not identify a common substrate or a shared role in infection. For these groups, I would first ask whether the original calls tracked cell number, morphology, or individual siRNA duplexes. A lower endpoint can reflect a real consequence of knockdown without revealing a direct dependency on the depleted protein.

## 4. What may be new

The **GPI-anchor pathway is the strongest candidate for a coherent but unexplained host phenotype**. C6 recovers both anchor initiation and attachment (PIGH, PIGY, PIGK), whereas the genes linking C6 to C1 are largely N-glycosylation factors (DDOST, STT3A, DPM1, MGAT1). That contrast gives a way to ask whether the GPI phenotype is distinct from general secretory dysfunction. I have relatively high confidence that the list captures GPI-anchor biology, but low confidence that it identifies a particular HIV-relevant cargo.

The **junction between Golgi traffic and surface-protein uptake** is the other lead I would keep. C1 is anchored by Golgi machinery (GOSR2, RAB2A, COG2), while GOSR2, ARF1, and AP2M1 link it to the sorting biology in C2, where CD4 appears. The interesting possibility is that some hits change the surface pool of CD4 without changing its total abundance. That is a more specific hypothesis than a general trafficking defect, but the network does not establish it, and it may depend on the cell model.

## 5. Priorities

1. **Determine whether the GPI phenotype is reproducible and selective.** Check whether independent siRNAs against the same genes agreed in the original screen. In uninfected cells, confirm that an on-target perturbation and rescue change surface GPI-anchored proteins, and compare that change with cell viability and an unrelated transmembrane cargo. This would distinguish a selective GPI defect from broader surface-protein loss.
2. **Find out whether the trafficking hits change receptor availability.** In uninfected cells, compare surface and total CD4 after perturbing representative Golgi and uptake genes (GOSR2, AP2M1), alongside an unrelated surface protein. A selective shift in surface CD4 with stable total CD4 would support a sorting explanation; broad cargo loss would point to a more general trafficking defect.
3. **Separate nuclear dependencies from dependence on the assay readout.** Test representative C3–C5 hits (MED14, NUP107, EXOSC3) against an unrelated, nonviral reporter with a matched output format. Compare reporter RNA and protein with cell number and, where informative, nuclear versus cytoplasmic RNA. If the same knockdowns depress an unrelated reporter, part of the original signal may come from how infection was scored; if they spare it, the hits become stronger candidates for an infection-specific explanation.
