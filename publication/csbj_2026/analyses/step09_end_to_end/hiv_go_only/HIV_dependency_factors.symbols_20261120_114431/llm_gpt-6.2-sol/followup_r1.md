## 1. Revised summary

The network is clearly distinguishable from random gene lists, and several of its strongest biological themes fit an siRNA screen for HIV dependency factors. The original estimate was partly right: this was a cell-based perturbation screen, but I mistook the prominence of membrane trafficking and glycosylation for evidence that the assay itself measured a surface or secretory product. With HIV as the endpoint, the receptor (CD4), nuclear pore subunits (NUP153, NUP358/RANBP2), and transcriptional machinery (MED14, MED4, MED6, MED7) make more sense together. The ER–Golgi and glycosylation findings are still intriguing, but their meaning depends more on the assay: they could reflect receptor availability, later events in the viral life cycle, or changes in the cells’ ability to produce the measured signal. The main question is no longer whether the list looks like a perturbation screen, but which hits reflect an HIV dependency rather than a dependency of the readout.

## 2. What fits

**Well established.** This is not in the network, but established HIV biology gives two particularly clear points of reference. First, HIV normally uses CD4 as its primary receptor. Its presence in C1 is therefore an immediate fit **if the screen retained CD4-dependent entry**. That qualification matters: if entry used a surrogate envelope that bypassed CD4, I would not interpret the CD4 hit as confirmation of the entry mechanism. Nor would I put the neighboring endocytic genes (AP2M1, HIP1R) on the same footing as CD4; they could affect receptor distribution or membrane turnover without defining how the virus entered.

Second, nuclear pore proteins have established roles in HIV infection, with particularly strong precedent for NUP153 and RANBP2. The recurrence of several scaffold subunits (NUP107, NUP133, NUP160, NUP85) makes C3 one of the most convincing findings in the screen: multiple perturbations point to the same cellular structure. But the pore finding is more specific than the label *export* suggests. Some of these proteins can matter before viral gene expression, whereas the later export of viral RNA is a separate event. The endpoint alone cannot place every pore hit at one step, and depletion of a scaffold subunit can have broader consequences than depletion of a particular transport factor.

**Plausible, but less specific.** C4 fits because expression from the provirus depends on host RNA polymerase II. What is compelling here is the recurrence of several Mediator subunits (MED14, MED4, MED6, MED7), rather than the presence of any one transcriptional regulator. What is less clear is whether that reflects a requirement at the HIV promoter or a broader loss of transcriptional capacity that would also lower an unrelated reporter. The RNA-processing portion of C1 presents a similar ambiguity: the exosome and RNA maturation factors (EXOSC3, EXOSC5, DHX33) may affect RNA output, but their presence does not identify a virus-specific RNA substrate.

C2 and C5 fit together as the machinery that moves and modifies membrane cargo (RAB1B, RAB2A, COG2–COG4; PIGY, PIGK, MGAT1). In a target-cell assay, one plausible explanation is altered surface composition, including receptor availability. If the endpoint also captured progeny production or spread, processing and delivery of newly synthesized viral envelope would become another plausible explanation. The distinction is important: a target-cell-only knockdown cannot explain a phenotype by changing how the incoming particles were made.

## 3. What does not fit

The **spindle genes in C1** are the clearest mismatch with a simple life-cycle interpretation. HIV does not require mitosis to gain nuclear access, so I would not infer a mitotic requirement from these hits. Some genes may matter through interphase functions—microtubule organization is a reasonable example (SPAST, MAP4)—while others could change proliferation or the state of the cells at the endpoint. A similar concern applies to broad transcription and RNA-processing hits: siRNA can leave cells viable yet substantially reduce their ability to make the assay signal.

The **GPI-anchor and glycolipid genes in C5** are unexpectedly specific. CD4 is not normally GPI-anchored, so the GPI genes (PIGY, PIGH, PIGK) are not a straightforward extension of the CD4 result. Altered membrane composition or an effect on another surface protein is plausible, but neither follows directly from the list. Likewise, the cullin adaptors in C6 (DCAF1, FBXW11, KLHDC2) do not point to a common substrate. DCAF1 has a well-known association with HIV-1 Vpr, but that precedent does not explain the entire group or establish why DCAF1 scored in this particular assay. The viral construct and endpoint matter for that interpretation.

## 4. What may be new

The most interesting lead is that **surface composition and membrane delivery may account for a distinct part of the screen phenotype**. The evidence is the convergence of coherent ER–Golgi machinery in C2 (RAB1B, RAB2A, COG2–COG4) with GPI anchoring and glycosylation in C5. The bridge gene MGAT1 is especially informative because it connects the two themes through Golgi N-glycan processing, a function distinct from GPI anchoring. I have moderate confidence that these are meaningful membrane-biology signals, but low confidence that they reflect one shared mechanism or act through CD4 specifically.

A second lead is a **membrane-organization phenotype that is not really about mitosis**. SPAST connects the spindle-associated genes in C1 to the trafficking and tubulin-related genes in C2; GOLPH3 independently connects Golgi traffic to cargo handling. That makes interphase cargo distribution a better hypothesis than a direct role for the mitotic spindle. It is still a lower-confidence lead than the ER–Golgi core: a few connecting genes can suggest an explanation without showing that it accounts for the screen phenotype.

## 5. Priorities

1. **Distinguish the nuclear-pore finding from general loss of cell function.** Reanalyze the original per-siRNA results for the pore genes against any available cell-count, viability, and control-reporter measurements. In uninfected cells, compare knockdowns using a generic nuclear transport readout alongside cell-health measurements. This would show whether several pore subunits yield a reproducible transport phenotype without a proportional collapse in cell health; it would not assign the original HIV phenotype to a particular stage.

2. **Find out what the membrane hits actually change at the cell surface.** In uninfected cells of the same type, use independent siRNAs to compare surface and total abundance of endogenous CD4, if expressed, alongside unrelated surface proteins and a general secretion readout. Compare representative trafficking and glycosylation hits (RAB1B, MGAT1, PIGY). A broad change in surface cargo would favor a general processing or delivery defect; a change confined to a subset of proteins would narrow the interpretation.

3. **Test whether the Mediator hits are unusually sensitive to the way the endpoint was measured.** Compare the original Mediator hit profiles with any matched control data, then use a nonviral reporter and endogenous RNA measurements in uninfected cells to estimate the effect of knockdown on general transcriptional output (MED14, MED4, MED6, MED7). If unrelated readouts fall to a similar extent, that would weaken the case for an HIV-specific interpretation. If they are largely preserved, the Mediator result becomes harder to dismiss as a generic reporter effect.
