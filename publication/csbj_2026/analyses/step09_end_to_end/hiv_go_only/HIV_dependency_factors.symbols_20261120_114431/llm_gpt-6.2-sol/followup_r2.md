## 1. Revised summary

The network is clearly distinguishable from matched random gene lists, and it makes more sense as an HIV host-factor screen than as a generic screen for surface protein abundance. My earlier estimate got the broad design right—a cell-based perturbation screen with a readout sensitive to several cellular systems—but missed infection as the phenotype. The clearest matches are the entry receptor and specific nuclear pore proteins (CD4, NUP153, RANBP2), which point to two distinct steps rather than a single pathway. The ER-to-Golgi and glycosylation findings are also credible, but their meaning depends on whether the assay measured infection of the initially exposed cells or included virus production and spread. The key distinction now is between genes HIV depends on and genes the *assay* depends on.

## 2. What fits

**Well established.** The relevant virology is not in the network itself, but CD4 is the HIV entry receptor, and HIV-1 uses specific nuclear pore proteins during early infection. That makes CD4 in C1 and NUP153 and RANBP2 in C3 particularly informative. The other pore subunits (NUP107, NUP133, NUP160, NUP85) reinforce the idea that pore architecture matters, though their appearance does not mean each one directly engages the virus. I would also revise my earlier emphasis on export: the presence of these nucleoporins is a better reason to think about **nuclear entry** than to infer a defect in viral RNA export. Those are different steps that happen at the same cellular structure.

C6 also has a specific precedent. HIV-1 Vpr recruits the host substrate adaptor DCAF1, so DCAF1 is more informative here than a general observation that ubiquitin ligases recur. That precedent does not establish why DCAF1 knockdown lowered the screen readout, or imply that the other adaptors (FBXW11, KLHDC2, KBTBD7) act through Vpr.

**Plausible but less specific.** The transcription machinery in C4 fits an infection readout that requires viral gene expression. But a group of core Mediator subunits (MED14, MED4, MED6, MED7) is not, by itself, evidence for a specific effect on HIV transcription: the same knockdowns could depress a reporter or change the cell’s transcriptional state more broadly. Likewise, C2 and C5 fit the biology of cells that must display entry factors or produce viral envelope proteins, but those interpretations are not interchangeable. In a target-cell-only assay, a defect in surface receptor availability is the more immediate explanation. If the endpoint includes virus production or spread, defects in protein maturation and trafficking become plausible on the producer side as well.

## 3. What does not fit

The spindle genes in C1 are the biggest surprise. HIV-1 does not generally require host-cell mitosis to gain nuclear access, so the combination of spindle-associated proteins (CSPP1, MAP4, SPAST) is not good evidence for a mitotic requirement. Knockdown could instead change cell number, cell-cycle distribution, or cell shape in ways that affect the endpoint. SPAST also has a credible role in membrane organization through microtubule remodeling, so its appearance is worth separating from the spindle signal rather than dismissing it as a fitness effect.

The GPI-anchor genes in C5 are another mismatch with a simple entry story: CD4 itself is not GPI-anchored. The combination of early anchor-synthesis components and the attachment machinery (PIGH, PIGY, PIGK) makes that branch worth taking seriously, but it does not identify the relevant surface protein. Similarly, the RNA-processing genes (EXOSC3, EXOSC5, DDX10) could reflect a real sensitivity to RNA handling or a broader loss of cellular and reporter output. In an siRNA screen, agreement among independent reagents and separation from cell-count effects would materially change how I read both findings.

## 4. What may be new

The most interesting possibility is that the screen is detecting **different kinds of surface-protein defects**. The trafficking machinery in C2 is unusually coherent (RAB1B, RAB2A, COG2, COG3, COG4), and MGAT1 bridges it to glycan processing in C5. GOLPH3 provides a separate bridge from Golgi traffic to cargo sorting in C1. I have moderate confidence that these genes point to a shared cell-biological phenotype, but low confidence that they identify a particular HIV dependency. The GPI-anchor branch is especially interesting because its effects could differ from those of a general ER-to-Golgi defect.

SPAST is the most interesting individual bridge. It ties the trafficking and microtubule findings in C2 to the spindle finding in C1, suggesting a way to explain an apparent cell-division hit through membrane organization instead. That is a plausible interpretation of the overlap, not evidence that HIV uses spastin directly. I would put more weight on it if the original SPAST phenotype were reproducible without a corresponding loss of cell number or a shift in cell-cycle distribution.

## 5. Priorities

1. **Separate surface delivery from protein abundance.** In uninfected cells of the same type, compare surface and total CD4 after perturbing representative trafficking and glycosylation hits (RAB1B, MGAT1, PIGY). Include an unrelated transmembrane cargo and a GPI-anchored marker. The comparison would tell us whether the hits reduce CD4 at the surface, broadly disrupt cargo delivery, or preferentially affect the GPI-anchored class.
2. **Separate pore function from reporter output.** Compare representative pore and Mediator hits (NUP153, NUP107, MED14) using a nonviral nuclear transport readout and a matched reporter-only control, alongside cell counts. If a perturbation depresses reporter output without a detectable transport phenotype, the original endpoint is harder to assign to a transport defect; the converse would support a distinct cellular phenotype without by itself assigning an HIV life-cycle step.
3. **Resolve the SPAST ambiguity.** In uninfected cells, ask whether independent SPAST reagents reproduce a cargo-localization phenotype and whether it persists when cells are compared within the same cell-cycle state. A reproducible trafficking defect without a matching proliferation defect would make SPAST more interesting than a generic spindle hit.
