## Executive summary

The network is not distinguishable from what random gene lists produce (empirical p = 0.49 for the size of the largest cluster). The one biological theme worth noting, tentatively, is how signals from antibodies and complement can tune B-cell responses (FCGR2B, CR2, C4BPB). The clearest inhibitory mechanism is antibody feedback through FCGR2B, but CR2 can *amplify* B-cell activation rather than restrain it. The unexpected gene is PARP3, which is better known for its role in DNA repair and does not fit that explanation as directly.

## C1 — Antibody feedback and complement sensing

The mechanistic distinction is not in the network itself, but it matters: when an antibody-containing immune complex engages both the B-cell antigen receptor and the inhibitory Fc receptor FCGR2B, FCGR2B dampens the activation signal. By contrast, CR2 recognizes complement fragments on an antigen and can lower the threshold for B-cell activation. C4BPB encodes the beta chain of a soluble complement-regulatory complex, so its connection to this theme is less direct than that of either receptor. PARP3 is harder to place alongside those mechanisms; the shared annotation does not establish that its DNA-repair role is part of the same response.

## Putting it together

The most useful connection is that antibodies and complement can change how a B cell responds to the *same antigen*. Antibody bound to an antigen can help recruit an inhibitory receptor (FCGR2B), while complement fragments deposited on an antigen can engage a co-receptor that helps the B cell respond (CR2). That makes these genes intelligible together without treating them as a single brake on immunity. Given the random-list comparison, this is a plausible connection among a few genes, not evidence that B-cell responses were broadly suppressed.
