### 1. Executive summary

The network is not distinguishable from what random gene lists produce (largest-cluster empirical p = 0.49). The one term that passed the term-level filter offers a tentative clue about how B cells respond to antibody- and complement-tagged material, but it does not establish a broader immune signature. The clearest biological contrast is between a receptor that can dampen B-cell activation and one that can make B cells more sensitive to an antigen (FCGR2B, CR2). That contrast is more informative than reading the result as evidence that B-cell immunity is suppressed.

### 2. C1 — Antibody feedback and complement sensing

The receptor mechanisms are not shown in the enrichment result, but they help explain why these genes can appear together. When an IgG-containing immune complex engages both the B-cell receptor and the inhibitory receptor FcγRIIB, the latter can dampen the activation signal (FCGR2B). By contrast, when the same antigen engages the B-cell receptor and carries a complement fragment recognized by CR2, the co-receptor can lower the threshold for activation (CR2). Complement regulation is an adjacent theme (C4BPB), though it is the alpha chains of the C4b-binding protein complex, not the beta chain encoded by C4BPB, that mediate its canonical control of C4b. The least obvious fit is PARP3, better known for its role in DNA repair; its presence is not enough to make DNA repair part of the same mechanism.

### 3. Putting it together

The useful thread is that a B cell can respond differently to an antigen depending on what is attached to it: antibody can provide inhibitory feedback, while complement can help the cell detect the antigen. The two receptor genes anchor that interpretation because their functions explain the contrast, not because either is a hub. There are no bridge genes to support a story linking this clue to another process.
